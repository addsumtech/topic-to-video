# Keyframe review

You see a film's three hero keyframes (`design/keyframes.jpg`), each with the line the voice says over it, and the
concept in one sentence. You did not make them, and that is the point: the designer knows what each frame is meant to
say, and that knowledge makes an unclear frame look clear.

First read each frame COLD for one second, before reading its line: write what it says to you. Then compare with
its line. For EACH frame, give a verdict and evidence:
- **clear and memorable** — your cold reading matches the line; one focal thing, at size; and it has an idea that
  could only belong to this subject, staged so a viewer would remember it;
- **clear but forgettable** — it says its line, but nothing in it would stay with a viewer (a fail: the film must
  not be mediocre);
- **unclear** — your cold reading misses the line, or something is wrong: unreadable type or screens, collisions,
  an element that does no job (a label repeating the caption, an ornament, a line nobody can decode), a screen tilted
  away while it should be read, a borrowed template (floating cards on a gradient, a feature grid, a pill timeline, a
  centred headline + subtitle, a logo wall).

Before the frames one by one, judge the film's STYLE against its subject: does its register fit what the film is
about (a real place, person or history told through its real photographs, maps and records; an invisible mechanism
designed and simulated; a product through its own screens and characters)? A real city's history as a toy diorama,
or a mechanism as stock photographs, is the first thing to name, with the register that would fit: no frame-level fix
repairs a style that does not belong to its subject (the Shenzhen film, 2026-11-20).

For every frame that is not clear and memorable, name the ONE change that would lift it most (bolder scale, a different camera, a
real light, a crop, a transformation, removing something), concretely enough to act on. Also say whether the three
frames feel like ONE film, whether the product's own protagonist (its characters, mascot, signature object) leads,
and, when a baseline film exists, whether each frame beats the baseline's frame for the same moment. Be specific and brief; no praise without a reason, no general advice. One round only: your
verdicts go back to the designer, who revises and decides.
