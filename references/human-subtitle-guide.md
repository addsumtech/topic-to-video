# Writing Natural, Human-Like Skill Subtitles

A subtitle (or tagline) should help someone immediately understand what
the skill does and why they should care. The best subtitles are usually
simple, concrete, and understated.

## 1. Explain, Don't Advertise

Avoid marketing language.

Instead of:

-   AI-powered intelligent assistant
-   Revolutionary productivity solution
-   Next-generation automation platform

Prefer:

-   Organize your notes in one place.
-   Find the files you're looking for faster.
-   Turn meeting notes into action items.

## 2. Focus on the User's Outcome

People care more about the result than the implementation.

Ask:

> "After using this skill, what becomes easier?"

Good examples:

-   Write reports from raw data.
-   Summarize long documents in minutes.
-   Compare multiple files side by side.

## 3. Be Specific

Specific language feels more trustworthy.

Weak:

-   Improve your workflow.

Better:

-   Turn screenshots into editable text.
-   Search across multiple PDFs.
-   Generate release notes from Git commits.

## 4. Use Everyday Language

Write like you're explaining the skill to a colleague.

Avoid unnecessary jargon unless your audience expects it.

## 5. Prefer Verbs

Verb-led subtitles are usually more natural.

Examples:

-   Find duplicate files.
-   Organize your research.
-   Track project progress.
-   Review pull requests faster.
-   Generate presentation slides.

## 6. Avoid Buzzwords

Reduce words such as:

-   AI-powered
-   Intelligent
-   Revolutionary
-   Cutting-edge
-   Best-in-class
-   Next-generation
-   Seamless

Only use them if they add meaningful information.

## 7. Don't Overclaim

Avoid promises that sound unrealistic.

Instead of:

-   Perfect every time.

Use:

-   Helps you...
-   Makes it easier to...
-   Designed to...
-   Quickly...
-   Automatically...

## 8. Keep It Short

A good subtitle is often between 5 and 12 words.

Examples:

-   Turn ideas into presentations.
-   Analyze logs without writing scripts.
-   Search your documents instantly.
-   Compare versions with clear summaries.
-   Automate repetitive spreadsheet tasks.

## 9. Make It Pass the Conversation Test

Imagine someone asks:

"What does this skill do?"

If your subtitle sounds like the first sentence you'd naturally answer
with, it's probably good.

## 10. A Simple Formula

Try one of these templates:

-   Verb + object
    -   Organize your research.
    -   Summarize meeting notes.
-   Verb + outcome
    -   Find information faster.
    -   Reduce repetitive work.
-   What it does + how
    -   Search documents using natural language.
    -   Generate diagrams from Markdown.
-   User outcome
    -   Spend less time formatting reports.
    -   Keep project documentation up to date.

## Quick Checklist

Before publishing, ask:

-   Can someone understand it in under 3 seconds?
-   Is it free of buzzwords?
-   Does it describe the user's benefit?
-   Is it concrete rather than abstract?
-   Would a human naturally say this aloud?
-   Is it under roughly 12 words?
-   Does it avoid unnecessary technical details?

If the answer is "yes" to most of these, the subtitle will usually feel
much more natural and human-written.
