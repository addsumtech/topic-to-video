# Shot language — a small vocabulary for purposeful coverage

Use these terms as a planning vocabulary, not a checklist. A shot may belong to several categories at once (for example, an insert shot can also be B-roll). Choose only what helps the story; don't force coverage into every film.

## Material and coverage
- **A-roll**: the primary narrative footage—presenter, interview, dialogue or main action. In narrated explainers, the voice/narration often acts as the A-roll even when no speaker is visible.
- **B-roll**: supplementary visuals that illustrate, contextualize, prove, contrast or add atmosphere while the main narration/dialogue continues. It can be real footage, animation, a diagram, a screen capture or a designed scene; label illustrative visuals honestly.
- **Cutaway**: briefly leave the main subject to show a related detail, environment or reaction. Useful for hiding edits, but it should add information or feeling.
- **Insert**: a close detail of something important to the action or claim (a hand, control, object, screen element, document). Keep readable details truthful.
- **Reaction shot**: show another person's response, or a character's response, to give a moment emotional or social meaning.
- **Establishing shot**: orient the viewer in a location, space or situation before closer action.
- **Pickup**: a supplemental shot recorded or generated later to repair a missing beat, continuity gap or detail.

A-roll/B-roll are functional categories, not separate visual styles or fixed timeline tracks. Don't default to a talking head plus generic stock footage: decide whether the subject is better carried by narration, real footage, diagrams, typography, screen captures, animation or a staged visual.

## Shot size and viewpoint
Use shot size to control context, intimacy and information density:
- **Wide / establishing**: geography, environment, scale or context.
- **Full / medium / close-up / extreme close-up**: progressively emphasize action, interaction, expression or detail.
- **Over-the-shoulder (OTS)**: place the viewer beside a participant looking toward a screen, object or conversation partner.
- **POV**: show what a character or operator sees; use sparingly when it clarifies experience.
- **Top-down / overhead**: spatial layout, process, objects or comparison.
- **Low / high angle**: suggest stature, vulnerability, observation or a useful spatial perspective—avoid arbitrary dramatic angles.

## Camera and action
Name a move only when it reveals, follows, connects or changes emphasis:
- **Pan / tilt**: rotate the camera horizontally / vertically.
- **Dolly / truck**: move the camera forward-back / laterally; this changes perspective.
- **Zoom**: change focal length from a fixed position; not the same as dolly.
- **Tracking / follow**: move with a subject.
- **Orbit / arc**: move around a subject to reveal spatial or relational change.
- **Static**: hold the frame when the image, performance or information deserves attention.
- **Handheld**: controlled instability, only when the tone or situation calls for it.

## Editing and transitions
- **Cut**: direct change between shots; it is valid and often best. Don't disguise every cut with an effect.
- **Jump cut**: remove time within a similar framing; useful for compression, but intentionally manage visual discontinuity.
- **Match cut**: connect shots through shape, position, movement, sound or meaning.
- **Cross-cut**: alternate between parallel events or storylines.
- **Montage**: compress time, build a progression, association or emotional rhythm through multiple shots.
- **J-cut**: the next scene's audio begins before its picture.
- **L-cut**: the previous scene's audio continues after the picture changes.
- **Cutaway / insert** can cover an A-roll edit, but the edit should still preserve meaning and audio continuity.

## Continuity cues
Across connected shots, preserve identity, prop state, screen direction, eyeline, action phase, geography, light direction and timing unless a deliberate change communicates something. For a multi-shot action, use a master/wide shot to establish geography, then tighter coverage to reveal meaningful beats; don't generate arbitrary coverage that cannot be edited together.

## Simple planning fields
For each planned shot, consider only the fields that matter:
`purpose`, `subject/action`, `shot_size`, `angle`, `camera_move`, `visual_type` (live/documentary, generated illustrative, diagram, UI capture, typography, 3D, etc.), `duration`, `transition`, `continuity_notes`, `audio`.
These are optional creative notes—not a rigid schema or mandatory checklist. The existing film plan/timeline remains the source of truth.
